# Source Quotes and Case Stories

Ammunition for pushing back when a step gets skipped. From Musk's public interviews (the Everyday Astronaut Starbase tour) and Walter Isaacson's biography.

## The Algorithm in Musk's Words

1. **Question the requirements.** "Make the requirements less dumb. The requirements are always dumb to some degree, no matter how smart the person who gave you those requirements. You have to start there, because otherwise you could get the perfect answer to the wrong question."
2. **Delete the part or process.** "If you're not forced to put back at least 10% of what you delete, you're not deleting enough. Most people feel like they've succeeded if they haven't been forced to put things back in. But actually they haven't, they've been overly conservative and left things in that shouldn't be there."
3. **Simplify and optimize.** "The most common mistake of smart engineers is to optimize a thing that should not exist. So you don't optimize until after you've tried to delete."
4. **Accelerate cycle time.** "Any given thing can be done faster than you think. But you shouldn't speed things up until you've tried to delete it and optimized it. Otherwise you're speeding up something that shouldn't exist."
5. **Automate.** "I've gone backwards so many times where I've automated something, sped it up, simplified it, and then deleted it. I got tired of doing that. So that's why I have this mantra."

On requirement ownership: "Each requirement should come with the name of the person who made it. Never accept that a requirement came from a department." Departments don't have to defend requirements. People do.

## Case Stories

**Fiberglass mats (delete the requirement).** Tesla had elaborate automated machinery placing fiberglass mats on battery packs. The stated requirement was fire safety. When actually tested, the mats did little for fire safety; the requirement had never been questioned. The entire carefully automated process was deleted.

**The orphan sensor (requirements need owners).** A Model 3 battery-pack sensor had "always been required." Nobody could name who required it; the original engineer had left years earlier, and the problem it solved no longer existed. Removed. Nothing broke.

**Radar deletion (delete what seems necessary).** The whole industry used radar for driver assistance, so radar was "required." Tesla deleted it and went cameras-only. Whatever one thinks of the outcome, the move illustrates the rule: if you never delete things that seem necessary, you never learn what is actually necessary.

**Falcon-wing doors (optimizing a thing that should not exist).** Model X doors: enormous engineering effort spent optimizing sensors, motors, and hinges. Musk later called the doors themselves a mistake. All that optimization was competent work in the wrong direction.

**Model 3 over-automation (automate last).** Tesla automated the production line before questioning, deleting, and simplifying the processes on it. The automation was too complex, kept breaking, and locked in bad processes. They ripped robots out and brought humans back. Musk: "Excessive automation at Tesla was a mistake. Humans are underrated."

## Why Smart People Run It Backwards

Smart people can make almost anything work better, so they can polish bad ideas and make wrong directions feel productive. Intelligence becomes a liability when it substitutes optimization for the harder question of whether the thing should exist. If everyone is optimizing and automating, the advantage is in questioning and deleting.
